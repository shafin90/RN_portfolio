Requirements are a system, not a document

Every failed mobile project I have been called into had a requirements document. That is the uncomfortable part. The document existed, it was reviewed, it was signed. It described every screen, every button, every happy path in careful detail. And then the product shipped, and money went missing, and seats got sold twice, and notifications never arrived, and nobody could explain why — because the failure never lived on a screen. It lived between screens, in the gaps the document never specified: the retry that fired twice, the payment that succeeded while the app showed failure, the booking that expired while the user was still typing their card number. The screens were correct. The system was not.

This is the central lesson of business analysis for mobile products. On the web, a requirements document can get away with describing pages and clicks, because the environment is forgiving: the connection is usually present, the session is usually alive, the server is one hop away and holds all the state. On a phone, none of that is true. The device is offline half the time, the operating system kills your app without asking, the clock cannot be trusted, the user denies the permission your flow depends on, and every request you send may arrive zero, one, or five times. A requirements document that does not specify behavior under those conditions is not a specification. It is a wish. And wishes do not survive contact with the operating system.

So business analysis for mobile is not a paperwork discipline. It is an engineering discipline done in words before it is done in code. The analyst's job is to force every stakeholder conversation through the hostile reality of the device until the requirements describe a system that can actually exist on a phone — with states, transitions, timeouts, retries, conflicts, and acceptance criteria that mention airplane mode. This post is how I do that: the questions I ask, the artifacts I demand, and the places where mobile requirements most often lie by omission.

The phone is a hostile environment. Say so in writing.

Start here, because everything else follows. A serious mobile requirements process opens by writing down the environment the software will actually run in, and refusing to let anyone pretend it is a desk with fiber internet. My environment statement for a typical consumer product reads something like this: the app will run on low-end Android devices with 2GB of RAM; on networks that drop for minutes at a time; with users who deny notification and location permissions; with device clocks wrong by hours; with the OS killing the app in the background to save battery; with users on OS versions five years old. Every line of that statement is a requirement in disguise, because each one deletes an assumption the happy path was built on.

Consider what each environmental fact demands. Intermittent connectivity demands that every mutating operation specify its offline behavior: does the booking queue locally and sync later, like offline ticket sales across 30+ cities that must reconcile per tenant without double-selling? Or does it refuse and say so, like a payment that must never complete without server verification? There is no default answer. "It should just work" is not a requirement; it is the absence of one, and whichever behavior the engineer guesses will be wrong for half your users. I built donation flows for villages with poor networks where the entire design centered on this question, and collaboration features for musicians where it did not matter at all — same company skill set, opposite requirements, and only the environment statement told us which was which.

OS kills demand that no critical state live only in memory. If the app is murdered mid-checkout — and on Android, it will be — what does the user return to? A lost cart, a resumed flow, a completed booking they never saw confirmed? Permissions denied demand fallback paths specified per permission: no location means manual city entry with normalized names, not a crash and not a blank screen. Wrong clocks demand that every time-sensitive rule name its time authority, which is always the server, never the device. Old OS versions demand a floor: which versions you support is a business decision with support-ticket consequences, and it belongs in the requirements, not in a bug report six months later. None of this is engineering trivia. Each item is a business decision about what the product promises, and if analysis does not extract the promise, engineering cannot keep it.

Specify state machines, not screens

Stakeholders think in screens. Systems think in states. The single highest-leverage move in mobile business analysis is translating every feature from a sequence of screens into a state machine before a single UI requirement is written. Take booking, the feature I have rebuilt most often. The screen version goes: search trips, pick a seat, enter details, pay, see confirmation. The state version goes: a seat is free, held, paid, confirmed, expired, or released — and the entire product is the set of transitions between those states, who is allowed to trigger each one, how long each one lasts, and what happens when two people trigger conflicting transitions at the same moment.

Why does this matter? Because screens hide the decisions and states expose them. The screen flow never asks who releases a held seat when payment fails. The state machine cannot avoid the question: the held state needs a timeout, the timeout needs a duration, the expiry needs an actor (a scheduled job, checked on read, or both), and the release needs to notify the waiting user or not — each a business decision with revenue attached. A hold that never expires is inventory permanently stuck; I have seen boats and buses lose real sales to exactly this, seats locked by abandoned checkouts that no requirement ever released. A hold that expires too fast abandons legitimate buyers mid-typing. The duration of a seat hold is not a technical detail. It is pricing, conversion, and customer anger expressed as a number of minutes, and analysis must put that number in the document with the stakeholder's signature next to it.

Conflicts are the same story. Two users tapping the last seat within the same second is not an edge case on a popular route; it is Tuesday. The requirement must state the resolution: first verified payment wins, the loser gets an instant refund and an apology screen, and the apology screen is itself a specified state, not an error toast someone invents during QA. Double-sell prevention, waiting lists, overbooking policy — these are business rules wearing engineering clothes. My rule for any analyst I work with: if a feature involves inventory, money, or capacity, the requirements contain a state diagram with every transition labeled, or the requirements are not finished. Screens come after, and they come easily, because a screen is just one state's clothing.

Every tap will be retried. Write that down.

Here is a fact about mobile networks that should be printed at the top of every requirements template: any request the app sends may be executed more than once, and neither the user nor the app will know. Timeouts lie. The user taps pay, the request reaches the server, the response dies on the way back, the app shows failure, the user taps again. Without explicit design, you have just charged them twice. This is not a rare catastrophe; on flaky networks it is routine traffic. So idempotency — the property that repeating an operation has the same effect as doing it once — is not an implementation detail for engineers to sort out later. It is a business requirement, because "a retry can never double-charge" is a promise the business makes to its customers, and the document must contain it verbatim.

What does that look like in practice? For every operation that moves money, inventory, or points, the requirements state three things. First, the identity of the operation: each user intent carries a unique key (an idempotency key, a client-generated transaction reference), so that five arrivals of the same tap resolve to one outcome. Second, the resolution rule: a repeated key returns the original result instead of performing the operation again — the second tap shows "already paid," never a second charge. Third, the user-visible behavior for each collision: what the screen says when the payment already succeeded, when the seat is already held by this same user, when the donation already recorded. I have applied this pattern to bus ticketing across dozens of cities, to boat bookings with fixed capacity, to multi-gateway payments with per-company accounting, and to donation receipts a donor must be able to trust. The domains differ. The requirement is identical, and it belongs in analysis, because only analysis can get the business to confirm that "never double-charge" outranks "never show a confusing message" when the two conflict — and they will conflict.

Refunds deserve the same treatment, because refunds are where retry logic goes to die. The requirement must state: refund requests are tracked by status, a retried refund resolves to the same refund record, partial refunds are either supported or explicitly out of scope, and every refund changes the booking state it belongs to — a refunded booking is not a confirmed booking with a footnote. Webhook-driven status (the payment provider telling you what actually happened, asynchronously, possibly twice, possibly out of order) must be named as the source of truth, with the app's optimistic display explicitly labeled as provisional. Donors, riders, and finance teams all read different screens; the requirements must state the one truth those screens share and which system owns it. If your document says "integrate the payment gateway" and stops, you have specified a logo, not a payment system.

Time is a business rule. Name its authority.

Nearly every mobile product runs on time, and nearly every requirements document treats time as decoration. Holds expire. Prices change at midnight. Trips depart. Promo codes die. Sessions time out. And every one of these is a small contract with the user that the product will either honor exactly or break visibly. The analysis failure I see most often is specifying durations without specifying clocks: "the hold lasts 10 minutes" — ten minutes according to whom? The device clock, which the user can change, which drifts, which sits in the wrong timezone? Or the server clock, which requires a round trip the offline user cannot make? A hold that expires by device time can be extended forever by toggling the clock. A hold that expires by server time needs a sync strategy for the offline buyer. Both are buildable. Neither is the default. The document must choose.

Then comes expiry behavior, the most skipped paragraph in mobile requirements. Every timeout needs four specified answers: what triggers the check (a scheduled sweeper, lazy evaluation on next read, a push from the server), what state the object moves to (held becomes released, never deleted — the audit trail is a business asset), what the user is told (a notification, an in-app banner on next open, silence), and what happens to the money (auto-refund initiated, refund status tracked, receipt issued). An expired booking that keeps the user's money is not a timeout; it is theft with a timer. An expired hold that silently releases while the user stares at a pay button is a support ticket with a timer. I specify expiry as carefully as purchase, because from the user's perspective expiry IS the product in the failure case — it is the only part of the timeout they will ever experience, and it had better feel designed.

Timezones and calendars are the quiet killers. A trip departing at 8 PM in one city and booked from another, a donation receipt dated for tax purposes, a program schedule spanning regions — each needs the document to state the canonical timezone (usually the event's local time, stored as UTC, displayed per device locale) and the display rule. Daylight-saving transitions will move your 2:30 AM trip into nonexistence once a year; that is a requirement to find in analysis, not a bug to find in production. My standing rule: any feature where a human makes plans around a clock gets a time section in the requirements with authority, canonical storage, display rule, and expiry behavior. It takes one page. It prevents the class of bug that makes users miss boats.

Identity is a requirement, not a login screen

Documents love login screens. They show the phone-number field, the OTP boxes, the cheerful success state, and they stop — as if identity were a door you pass through once. On mobile, identity is the foundation every other feature stands on, and the requirements must treat it as infrastructure: who the user is, how the app knows across reinstalls and devices, what each role may touch, and how access dies. Get this wrong and you do not have an auth bug; you have a business where drivers see the wrong company's trips, where refunded users keep premium access, where a stolen phone keeps spending.

The first analysis question is what identity even means for this product. For a ride-hailing driver app, the phone number is the identity — it survives reinstalls, it matches the dispatch records, it works for users who have never owned an email address. For a two-sided marketplace, registration must provision half a dozen things at once: the profile, the chat identity, the notification tokens, the initial skills or preferences — and the requirements must state the atomicity: if step four of six fails, is the user registered or not? I have built both, and the document that says "user signs up with phone OTP" without specifying provisioning order, partial-failure behavior, and what the support agent sees, has specified a demo, not a system. Guest checkout sharpens the point further: a booking made without an account still needs an owner for refunds, receipts, and disputes, so the requirements must define the guest identity (phone number plus booking reference, say) and its upgrade path when the guest later registers. Every guest is a future support ticket; specify them now.

Roles and revocation close the loop. Hierarchical roles — platform admin, company admin, staff, driver, customer — each with explicit data boundaries: a company admin sees their own trips, never the neighbor company's; a staff member refunds but never reconfigures payouts. The requirements state the matrix, because "admin can manage bookings" will be interpreted five ways by five engineers. And revocation must be specified as a first-class flow, not assumed: when a driver is deactivated, do their active sessions die immediately or at next check? Are their tokens revoked server-side or merely deleted from the device (which the thief still holds)? Does the dashboard show the kill switch and its audit log? Access that cannot be revoked on demand is not access control; it is hope with a password field. Analysis writes the revocation requirement. Engineering builds the middleware. The founder sleeps.

Notifications are a delivery contract, not a feature

"The app should send push notifications" is the most expensive sentence in mobile requirements, because it reads as one feature and hides six. A serious notification requirement specifies the contract: which events notify, through which channels, with what fallback, linking where, counted how, and what happens when delivery fails. Miss any clause and the OS will fill it in — with silence. The failure is uniquely cruel: nothing crashes, no ticket is filed, the user simply never comes back, and the team debates message copy for messages that were never received. The copy was never the problem. The pipe was.

Channel strategy comes first. Push is the primary channel but the least reliable one: tokens expire, users disable permissions, manufacturers kill background services, iOS caps local scheduled notifications (I have built around the 60-item ceiling by rebuilding six-week reminder plans from program schedules, and the requirement had to state the horizon, the lead time, and the rebuild triggers — none of which appear in "send reminders"). So the requirements name the fallback per message class: booking confirmations fall back to SMS when push cannot deliver; marketing never does; OTP codes use SMS first with push as backup. Each fallback has a cost — per-message SMS pricing is a business number — so channel priority is a budget decision disguised as a technical one, and analysis must surface the price tag before launch, not after the first invoice.

Then the payload contract: every notification names its deep-link target (the exact chat, the exact booking, the exact trip — "opens the app" is a failed requirement), its badge semantics (whose unread count, synced from where, cleared by what action), its timing rules (quiet hours, timezone of delivery, expiry — a "your bus leaves in 10 minutes" delivered an hour late is misinformation), and its failure handling (dead tokens pruned and logged, ticket statuses tracked, undeliverable critical messages escalated to SMS). Dual-channel delivery — push plus email, or push plus in-app inbox — must state the dedup rule so the user is informed once, not spammed thrice. I specify notifications the way lawyers specify SLAs: event, channel order, fallback, target, timing, and proof of delivery. Because that is what they are. The retention curve your investors will stare at is built out of these clauses.

"Good search" must be measurable, or it is not specified

Search requirements are where business language goes vaguest — "fast, relevant search" — and where vagueness costs most, because search is the front door of discovery products and users judge it in one try. Serious search requirements decompose into three measurable promises: matching (nothing that should match is missing), ranking (the right things come first), and latency (results arrive before patience expires). Each gets numbers. Matching gets a test corpus: these fifty queries must return these items, including misspellings, French and English variants across African markets, inconsistent location names normalized to canonical cities. Ranking gets rules: proximity weighted against rating how? New listings boosted for how long? Paid placement labeled and separated from organic — a business decision with legal consequences, stated in analysis, not discovered in an app-store review. Latency gets a budget: first results in under 400 milliseconds on a mid-range device on 3G, with the payload sizes and index strategy that budget implies.

The mobile-specific clauses matter most. Search must specify its offline behavior (cached recent searches? a browsable subset? an honest empty state?), its per-keystroke cost (debounced queries against a local index versus server round trips that burn data plans), and its empty and failed states as designed screens with specified copy and next actions — because "no results" is the moment the user decides whether your product understands them, and a blank page is a decision too. Filters are business rules: which facets exist, which combinations are valid, what "near me" means in kilometers per market. I have built discovery across musician skills with proximity ranking, campaign matching across employment and eligibility attributes, and city-route-station lookup across dozens of tenant-isolated cities. Every one of them reduced, after the first month of complaints, to one of three sentences: something is missing, the order is wrong, or it is too slow. Requirements that name all three sentences before launch get few complaints after it.

Acceptance criteria that survive the operating system

All of the above converges on one artifact: acceptance criteria written for the device that exists, not the demo that flatters. The standard Given-When-Then format works — provided the givens are allowed to be hostile. "Given the user is on the payment screen" is a weak given. "Given the user is on the payment screen with airplane mode on," "given the app was killed after the payment request was sent," "given the user double-taps the pay button," "given the session expired while the form was open" — these are the givens that separate specified software from hopeful software. My rule: every story that moves money, inventory, or identity carries at least one hostile given, or it goes back to analysis.

Concrete examples, from real documents. Booking: "Given a seat is held with 3 minutes remaining, when the payment succeeds at minute 4, then the booking is confirmed only if the hold is still valid; otherwise the payment is auto-refunded, the seat is released, and the user sees the seat-unavailable screen with alternative trips." That single criterion forced decisions about hold duration, expiry checks, refund initiation, and the apology screen — four business decisions hiding inside one user story. Notifications: "Given the device token is stale, when a booking confirmation is triggered, then the system sends SMS within 60 seconds and logs the push failure; the user receives exactly one message." That criterion forced channel priority, fallback timing, dedup, and observability. Authentication: "Given the user reinstalls the app, when they verify the same phone number, then their bookings, receipts, and notification preferences are restored without contacting support." That criterion forced the identity model, the guest-upgrade path, and the backup strategy. Each criterion is a paragraph. Each paragraph prevented a class of production incident. This is the highest-ROI writing in the entire project, and it happens before a line of code.

Non-functional criteria need the same hostility. Performance: "search returns first results within 400ms on the reference low-end device on throttled 3G" — with the reference device named, because "fast on my flagship" is how latency budgets die. Reliability: "no confirmed booking is ever lost across app kill, reinstall, or 24 hours offline; the booking is visible on next launch after sync." Security: "a revoked driver token is rejected within 60 seconds; deactivation from the dashboard propagates without requiring app update." Usability under constraints: "every critical flow completes with notification permission denied, location denied, and dark mode on." Read that last one again — it costs nothing to write and catches an entire category of assumption. The QA team will bless you. More importantly, the users on $80 phones in low-signal villages — the users most products claim to serve and fewest specify for — will actually be able to use what you built.

Why mobile estimates explode, and how analysis defuses them

Founders ask why the estimate doubled between the proposal and the plan. The honest answer is the state matrix: the requirements described one path, and engineering priced the matrix. Every feature on mobile multiplies across devices (flagship to five-year-old budget phones), OS versions (each with different permission models, background limits, and quirks), network conditions (online, offline, flapping, captive portals), permission states (granted, denied, denied-forever, revoked mid-flow), and app states (foreground, background, killed, Russiagate — I mean, updated mid-session). Nobody builds the full cross product. But the estimate must account for deciding, per feature, which cells are supported, which degrade, and which are explicitly out of scope — and that decision work is business analysis, not engineering padding.

The scoping tool I use is the explicit non-goal. For each feature, the document states what it will not do, with the stakeholder's initials: no offline ticket sales in phase one (with the revenue implication stated); no partial refunds (with the support-script implication stated); no support below Android 10 (with the excluded-user percentage stated). Non-goals convert silent assumptions into priced decisions, and they are where estimates shrink without quality shrinking — because half the matrix was never business-critical, it was just unspecified, and unspecified work gets built anxiously and tested never. The other estimator's friend is the boring-states inventory: for every screen, the requirements name its loading, empty, error, offline, and permission-denied states, or mark them inherited from a shared pattern. Five states times twenty screens is a hundred small designs; unlisted, they surface during QA as a hundred arguments. Listed, they take an afternoon. This is what "analysis reduces cost" actually means in practice: not cheaper hours, but fewer surprises, and surprises are the most expensive line item in any mobile budget.

The one-page checklist

If this post had to compress into the single page I tape to the wall at project kickoff, it would be these questions, answered in writing before design starts. What is the environment — devices, networks, OS floor, permissions reality? What are the states of every inventory, money, and capacity object, with all transitions, actors, and timeouts labeled? Which operations are idempotent, what carries the operation identity, and what does the user see on collision? Where does time come from, what expires, who checks expiry, and what happens to the money and the message when it does? What is identity — including guests, roles, provisioning atomicity, and revocation latency? What is the notification contract — events, channel order, fallback costs, deep-link targets, badge rules, quiet hours? What are the search numbers — matching corpus, ranking rules, latency budget on a named reference device? Which acceptance criteria carry hostile givens — airplane mode, killed app, double-tap, expired session? And what is explicitly out of scope, with the business cost of each exclusion stated? Nine questions. Answer them, and the document describes a system. Skip them, and it describes a demo — and demos, as every founder learns exactly once, do not process payments.

The deeper point is about who business analysis serves. It does not serve process; it serves the user holding a cheap phone in a dead zone with money at stake, and the founder whose business must be correct for that user on the first try — because on mobile there is rarely a second try. The app that double-charges is deleted. The ticket that never arrives is bought elsewhere next time. The notification that never lands is a customer quietly gone. Requirements are where those outcomes are decided, months before code, in plain sentences, by people willing to ask hostile questions of friendly screens. Write them like an engineer. Sign them like a founder. The operating system will test them either way.

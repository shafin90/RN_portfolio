A seat is a lock

The failure is quiet, which is what makes it expensive. A crashed screen produces a report. A failed payment produces a support ticket. A seat sold twice produces nothing at all until the boat reaches the dock and someone is standing there with a ticket for a seat that no longer exists. Nothing in the system says the word error. The system reported success, twice, faithfully.

Once I stopped treating a seat as a number on a screen and started treating it as a lock, the whole feature changed shape. A seat is not free or sold. A seat is free, held, taken, cancelled, or freed again, and every one of those transitions has an owner, a clock, and a rule for what happens when two people try to move it at the same moment. That is the entire engineering. Everything else is the screen that reports it.

The lock must have a life

The first version of any booking flow is optimistic, and optimistic is always wrong in the same way. Someone taps a seat, and the seat becomes theirs immediately. Then they close the app, or their connection drops, or they get distracted with a card number, and the seat stays locked indefinitely. I have seen buses and boats lose real sales to exactly this: inventory stuck behind abandoned checkouts that no requirement ever released.

So a hold needs an expiry, and that expiry is not a technical detail. It is pricing expressed as minutes. Too long and you sell phantom inventory. Too short and you break a genuine buyer who was mid-typing. The duration belongs in the requirements document with the founder's signature beside it, because it is a conversion decision wearing an engineering costume.

When a hold expires, someone has to notice. That someone is either a scheduled sweeper, a lazy check on read, or both, and I build all three. The sweeper releases stale holds on a timer so inventory recovers even if nobody is looking at it. The lazy check refuses to sell a seat whose hold has already died. And the release has to be visible to the person who was waiting, or they will tap a seat that was gone a minute ago and conclude the app is broken.

Two people, one seat, the same second

On a popular route this is not an edge case. It is Tuesday. The requirement has to state the resolution before it happens, because the engineer who implements it will otherwise guess, and the guess will be wrong for half your users. My rule is that the first verified payment wins, the second person gets an immediate, unambiguous answer, and that answer is a specified state rather than an error toast someone invents during QA.

Double-sell prevention, waiting lists, and overbooking policy are business rules wearing engineering clothes. Which is why the seat has to be decremented inside the same database transaction that records the payment. If the ticket is written in one transaction and the seat is decremented in another, then somewhere between those two transactions a crash leaves you with money and no seat, or a seat and no money. Neither is acceptable, and both are extremely common when the two writes live in different places.

The retry that charges twice

Here is a fact about mobile networks that should be printed at the top of every booking template. Any request the app sends may execute more than once, and neither the user nor the app will know. The timeout lies. The user taps pay, the request reaches the server, the response dies on the way back, the app shows failure, the user taps again. Without explicit design, you have just taken two payments for one seat. On a weak radio this is not a rare catastrophe. It is routine traffic.

So idempotency is a business requirement, not an implementation detail for engineers to sort out later. Each booking intent carries a unique key. A repeated key returns the original result instead of performing the operation again, and the second tap shows something like already paid, never a second charge. I have applied exactly this pattern to bus ticketing across dozens of cities, to boat bookings with fixed capacity, to multi-gateway payments with per-company accounting, and to donation receipts a donor has to be able to trust. The domains are entirely different. The requirement is identical.

The offline seller

Real transport sells tickets where there is no signal, at a counter, on cash. This breaks most booking architectures immediately, because they assume a request can reach the server before a decision is made. The seller has to keep selling. So the sale is written locally with a pending flag, and it joins the backend when it can, batched so one company's queue is never starved by another's rush.

Then conflicts arrive, because two offline sellers can hold the same seat without ever seeing each other. The resolution has to be stated in advance: the server decides, and the phone re-renders quietly when it learns the truth. The seller is not told they were wrong at the counter in front of a customer. The system reconciles, and the customer gets one seat.

What this buys

None of this is visible when it works, which is exactly why it is the product. The owner never opens a spreadsheet at midnight to find out which seats are stuck. Nobody is turned away at the dock. Finance can close the day because every payment resolves to exactly one booking. The screen was the easy part. The lock, its life, and its owner were the work.

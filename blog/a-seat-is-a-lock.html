A seat is a lock

Founders see a map of chairs. The product is whether two phones can buy the same row — and whether money and the ticket ever disagree.

Two phones, one seat

Every booking screen is a drawing that has to stay consistent under conditions the drawing never shows. The user taps a seat. Another user taps the same seat a fraction of a second later on a worse network. A payment gateway takes its time. A webhook arrives twice. Someone kills the app on the confirm screen and opens it again tomorrow. None of this is an edge case. On a bad network, all of it is the normal case, and the system has to produce exactly one ticket and exactly one charge every time.

The failure that kills trust is the double-sell: two passengers, one seat, settled at the boarding gate or the dock in front of everyone. It does not matter whose fault it was — the network, the gateway, the retry logic. The passenger holding the invalid ticket tells the story, and the story is that your product cannot be trusted with money. One public double-sell costs more than a month of infrastructure work, because it converts every future buyer into someone who screenshots their receipt and arrives early out of fear.

The second failure is quieter but just as expensive: stuck inventory. A user taps a seat, the payment fails or the app dies, and the seat stays reserved forever. Nobody owns it, nobody can buy it. A boat leaves with empty seats that were paid for by nobody. An event looks sold out while money sits uncollected. Stuck inventory is what happens when a team builds the hold path and forgets the release path, and it compounds silently until someone in operations notices the numbers do not add up.

Both failures come from the same modeling mistake: treating the chair on the screen as the state. If your model is "the client reserved a chair," you will oversell and you will strand inventory. The chair is a picture. The lock is the state.

The lock is the state

A lock is a record that says a specific seat is held for a specific buyer until a specific deadline, and nothing else can touch it until then. When the first phone taps, the backend creates that record with an expiration. When the second phone taps the same seat, it does not get a race — it gets an answer: taken. The entire booking flow downstream only has to reason about one thing, which is whether the lock is valid, expired, or converted into a ticket.

The expiration is the whole design problem in miniature. Too long, and abandoned carts freeze inventory during peak sale hours — the exact hours when every frozen seat is lost revenue. Too short, and a legitimate buyer on a slow connection loses the seat mid-payment and learns that your checkout punishes people with bad networks, who are usually most of your market. The right lifetime is measured in minutes, matched to how long your slowest real payment path takes, with a small margin. It is a business decision disguised as a constant, and it should be configurable without a deploy, because the right value on launch day is never the right value during festival season.

Locks must release themselves. Any design where release depends on the client saying goodbye is broken, because clients never say goodbye — they lose signal, run out of battery, and get killed by the OS. Expiration in the store itself, enforced by the store and not by application code remembering to clean up, is what guarantees release. On top of that, a reaper that reconciles expired holds against payment state catches whatever the expiration misses, such as holds created in the seconds before a deploy or a failover. Belt and suspenders sounds unglamorous until the night it saves a full sailing.

Scope the lock to the right boundary. On a multi-tenant platform, a lock for one company's bus must be invisible to every other company's queries — lock keys carry the tenant, and every read and write carries it too. A lock that leaks across tenants is a double-sell wearing a different hat. The same applies to the event records that track payment notifications: scoped per company, so replaying or redelivering a notification can never cross from one business into another.

Never commit before confirmed money

The rule that governs everything downstream is simple: a reservation is never completed before payment is verified. Not initiated, not authorized, not "the gateway said okay on the client" — verified, on the server, against the gateway, with a signature or status the backend trusts. The ticket row is created inside the same database session as the verified payment, so the two can never disagree. There is no window in which a ticket exists without money, and no window in which money exists without a ticket. Every horror story in booking lives in one of those two windows.

Client-side payment callbacks are hints, not facts. SDKs report success when the user experience succeeded, which is not the same as the money moving. Gateways retry, webhooks delay, and users close the app between the two. So the flow is always reserve, then pay, then confirm — three distinct states, each recoverable. Reserve creates the lock. Pay collects money against the lock's identity. Confirm converts the lock into a ticket only when verification passes. Anything that fails or expires in the middle auto-cancels and releases the seat instead of leaving inventory stuck.

Guest checkout sharpens all of this, because there is no account to absorb ambiguity. When a booking belongs to a phone number or an email instead of a user row, the idempotency key becomes the identity of the transaction: same key, same transaction, no matter how many times the request arrives. A retry is then always safe by construction. The user can tap pay three times on a stalling connection and still be charged once, which is the behavior that turns anxious first-time buyers into repeat customers.

Killing the app mid-flow must be boring. On relaunch, the client asks the server what exists for its pending key: lock still valid means resume payment, converted means show the ticket, expired means start over with an apology instead of a spinner. Reconciliation on launch is a small feature with an outsized effect on support load, because the users most likely to kill the app are the ones on the worst devices, who are also the ones least likely to email support and most likely to leave a one-star review.

Webhooks are events, not commands

Payment notifications arrive twice, arrive late, and arrive out of order. A design that processes each delivery as a fresh instruction will double-confirm, double-refund, or resurrect cancelled bookings. The fix is to treat every notification as an idempotent event: each carries a stable identity, each identity is recorded exactly once, and reprocessing the same identity is a no-op that returns the current state instead of doing the work again.

The dedup record must live in the same transactional boundary as the state change it guards. Check-then-act across two systems is a race; a unique constraint on the event identity inside the booking database turns the race into a single atomic decision. The loser of the race gets told the current status, which is genuinely useful — the second delivery of a success notification becomes a free status query instead of a second charge.

Out-of-order delivery is the subtler half. A refund notification can arrive before the success notification it refers to, especially across gateway retries and queue redeliveries. State machines survive this; flags do not. Model the payment as an explicit lifecycle — pending, verified, failed, expired, refunded, partially refunded — with legal transitions, and let events move it along the graph or be ignored when they describe a state already passed. A status column that anything can overwrite is how money and tickets start disagreeing at 2 AM.

Verify before you trust. Every notification is signature-checked on the server before it touches state, using secrets the client never sees. Order creation and verification both happen server-side, and the booking lifecycle only advances on verified facts. This sounds obvious and is skipped constantly, usually in the rush before launch, usually discovered during the first fraud attempt rather than before it.

Refunds are policy, not favors

Refunds belong in the same story as booking from day one, because the moment of maximum emotion in a ticketing product is not purchase — it is cancellation. Time-to-departure is the natural policy axis: full refund before a distant cutoff, a step-down as departure approaches, nothing after. Encode it as rules the system executes, not judgment calls staff make. If refunds live in a chat tool, you do not have a payments product. You have an operations habit that will not survive the first angry thread, and every exception granted in chat becomes a precedent the next hundred customers cite.

Refund execution is asynchronous work, not a button handler. It goes into a queue with retry limits, because gateways fail refund calls exactly as often as they fail anything else, and a refund that dies halfway is a support ticket with a deadline attached. Each attempt is idempotent against the same event identity, statuses are tracked per attempt, and anything that exhausts retries lands in a visible dead-letter view for a human instead of vanishing. Failed and expired payments trigger the same machinery in reverse: booking cleanup and seat release, automatic, no human in the loop.

Partial states deserve first-class handling. A donation platform tracks pending, success, failed, and refunded as distinct, exportable states because finance reconciles against them monthly. A transport operator needs cancelled bookings with refund tracking separate from completed travel, plus per-boat occupancy and revenue that already excludes refunded seats. Reports that mix these together look fine in a demo and poison every business decision made from them afterward.

The invariant to defend is one status per payment, visible to the customer, support, and finance alike. When those three see different truths, the company pays three times: the refund, the support hours, and the trust. Backward compatibility matters here too — when a gateway contract changes, the old flow keeps working while the new one rolls out, because money paths are the last place to discover a breaking change.

The client is a citizen, not the source of truth

The mobile UI participates in this contract without owning it. It holds the lock identity for the session, disables the pay button the moment a payment is in flight, and treats every retry as a safe repeat of the same key rather than a new attempt. Double-tap protection belongs on the client and the server both, because either one alone has a failure mode the other covers: the client prevents the accidental second tap, the server makes the deliberate replay harmless.

Receipts are a product surface, not paperwork. On a poor network, the donor or passenger must be able to trust the receipt more than the spinner — verification status, webhook-driven updates, and a receipt that arrives even when the confirmation screen never loads. Manual verification paths, like receipt uploads for bank transfers, close the loop for payment methods the gateway never sees. Automated confirmations with payment instructions remove the round trip that otherwise eats support mornings.

Rollouts deserve the same caution as payments. New booking and collaboration paths ship behind feature flags and prove out on a slice of traffic before they touch the whole base — never the whole base before the moment that matters, whether that is an investor demo or a holiday rush. Tying new flows to notifications and conversations from the start means the new path is exercised end to end while it is still small enough to watch.

Peak load is a separate system

Everything above is tested by volume, and volume arrives all at once: festival sales, route openings, the hour a popular sailing goes live. Hundreds of buyers contend for the same inventory in the same minute, on the worst networks of the year, with retries multiplying every tap. A booking flow that is correct at ten requests a second and collapses at a hundred is not a booking flow — it is a demo with a database.

The sale path must never block on anything that is not the sale. Confirmations, receipts, notifications, analytics, dashboard updates — all of it leaves the request path through async queues and gets delivered without holding the buyer's tap hostage. The lock check and the lock write stay fast and small; everything heavy happens after the answer is already on its way back to the phone. When confirmations ride queues instead of the request, a slowdown in email delivery degrades receipts, not revenue.

Contention needs fairness, not just speed. Locks serialize access to a seat by nature, which means the lock store is the hottest key in the system during a rush. Keep lock operations to single atomic commands with expiration attached, never read-modify-write cycles across round trips. Per-tenant scoping does double duty here: one company's flash sale cannot starve another company's ordinary Tuesday, because their locks, queues, and event records never share a lane. And retries during congestion must back off rather than hammer — a client that retries instantly on timeout turns a slow minute into a dead ten minutes for everyone.

Capacity planning for booking is really abandonment planning. Most locks created during a rush will never convert — browsers, comparers, the curious. Size the expiration and the reaper for the abandonment rate, not the purchase rate, and watch the ratio of locks created to tickets confirmed as a first-class metric. When that ratio spikes, something upstream changed: a confusing fare screen, a gateway slowdown, a bot. The metric tells you before the support inbox does.

Questions to ask before you fund a booking flow

Founders evaluating a booking product, or a team proposing to build one, can get most of the risk on the table with six questions. What is the lock lifetime, and can operations change it without a deploy? What identifies a transaction across retries — and what happens when the app is killed mid-payment? Which ticket states exist, and which transitions are legal? How are duplicate gateway notifications deduped, and in the same transaction as what? What is the refund policy as executable rules, and where do exhausted retries surface? What does the guest path do differently from the signed-in path?

A team with crisp answers is holding the right model; the demo almost does not matter. A team that answers in terms of screens — the seat map, the checkout page, the success animation — is describing the drawing, not the system. Booking looks like a map of chairs. It is a distributed lock, a payment that lies, and a refund policy that has to fire without a human in the loop. Build those three, and the chairs take care of themselves.

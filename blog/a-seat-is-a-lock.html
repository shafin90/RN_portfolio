A seat is a lock

A double-sold seat is the only bug in my line of work that a customer can physically hold while you explain it to them. There is no crash report, no support ticket, and no error anywhere in your logs. The system reported success twice, faithfully and correctly, because from where it sat, both sales were valid. You find out at the dock, or at the gate, standing in front of the person who has a ticket for a seat that no longer exists.

The refund is not what hurts. The refund is small and calculable. What hurts is the refusal, the driver's morning, the support hours, and the message that eventually reaches you as an owner: we overbooked again. That message is the actual invoice, and it arrives with your name on it.

It happens because a seat was modelled as a number

A number gets read and written. A lock gets acquired, held, and released, with an owner and an expiry. The difference sounds like vocabulary until you watch what happens without it. If a seat is a plain number, then when someone taps it, the sensible implementation marks it theirs straight away, and the moment that person closes the app, switches networks, or gets distracted halfway through a card number, that seat is locked to a stranger indefinitely. Nothing releases it, because nothing in the system was ever told there was a lock to release.

I have watched real revenue disappear this way. Not dramatically. A seat held by an abandoned checkout at ten in the morning is still held at noon, still at closing, still tomorrow. It shows up as an empty bus and nobody can explain why, because the number in your dashboard says it was sold.

Three decisions that belong to you

Here is the part that is genuinely yours rather than your engineer's. Each of these is a business decision disguised as a technical setting, and if you do not make it explicitly, your team will make it for you, silently, and they will guess wrong.

How long is a hold worth? This is a number of minutes, and it is your pricing decision expressed as a setting. Too long and you sell inventory that does not exist. Too short and you break honest customers who were typing their card number. There is no technically correct answer. There is only the answer that matches how your customers behave, and only you know that.

Who wins when two people want the last seat? This happens on a busy route roughly as often as you would guess, and it is not an edge case, it is a normal day. The answer has to be stated before it happens: the first verified payment wins, the second person gets a clear next option immediately, and that option is a designed state rather than an error message someone invents during testing on the night of a launch.

What happens to the money when the seat is gone? When a payment succeeds and the booking cannot complete, the refund has to begin without anyone noticing. Not when support receives a complaint. Not at the end of the week. Automatically, and visibly, so your finance team sees a pending refund rather than a missing one. An expired booking that keeps the customer's money is not a bug. It is theft with a timer on it.

What engineering owes those decisions

Once you have made those three calls, the engineering job is to make them physically true rather than merely intended. Ticket and seat decrement in the same database transaction, so no crash between two writes can leave you with money and no seat or a seat and no money. Every booking carries a unique key, so a customer on a weak connection who taps pay three times produces one booking and one charge, with the later taps finding the first result instead of creating new ones. Anything that moves money, inventory, or a voucher repeats safely, and the product never promises a customer twice.

Then the case that breaks most architectures: the sale at the counter, on cash, with no signal. Your operation still has to sell. That sale is recorded locally and joins your systems when it can, and when two offline sellers happen to hold the same seat without ever seeing each other, the server decides and the phone quietly corrects itself. The customer is not told they were wrong at the counter. The system reconciles, and the customer gets one seat.

What this looked like in practice

A river boat with a fixed number of seats, where the cost of a mistake is not a percentage point but a passenger standing on the dock. That business leans hard toward safety, because being wrong is public and immediate. A bus network across dozens of cities, where each company settles its own day and its own money, leans toward separation, because one company's rush must never corrupt another company's books. A donation flow on a village connection leans toward a single provable outcome, because a person who is charged twice for a donation does not retry. They leave, and they tell other people.

Same state machine, same guarantees, three different temperaments, because three different businesses are absorbing the failure.

Three questions for whoever builds this for you

If you take one thing from this, take the questions rather than the conclusions, because the conclusions depend on your business and the questions do not. Ask what happens to a seat when the person holding it closes the app. Ask what a customer sees if they are the second person to want the last one. Ask what a customer sees if their payment succeeds but the booking cannot complete, and how long until anyone on your team would notice if the answer were wrong.

If the answers are immediate and specific, the system was designed. If the answers are that it depends, or that it has not come up yet, then you have a demo. Demos, as every founder learns exactly once, do not run at the dock.

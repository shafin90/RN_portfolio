Your search is not broken. It is one of three failures.

Teams do not under-invest in search. They over-invest in the wrong layer, and they do it because the symptom looks the same from every angle. The list feels wrong, so somebody rebuilds the text box. It still feels wrong, so somebody adds a ranking model. It is still wrong, and now the ranking model is the thing blamed, and the engineers who built it quietly stop mentioning it in standup.

After a lot of these, I settled on something that has saved more time than any query I have written. Search fails in three places and only three. Something that should match does not. The wrong thing ranks first. Or the right thing arrives too late. Every complaint I have ever received reduced, after the first month of noise, to one of those three sentences. The discipline is to work out which sentence you are living in before writing any code, because each points at a different part of the system, and the fix for one is worthless against the other two.

Nothing matches that should

This failure is almost never the database. The database is doing exactly what it was asked. The user is searching for a city they have typed before, spelled differently, in a language that is not the one the data was entered in, or looking for a skill that their own vocabulary does not share with yours. If matching requires exact words, a correct search returns an empty result, and an empty result reads to a person as an absence rather than a mismatch.

The fix is not a cleverer query. It is cleaning the input once, at the moment it enters the system, instead of forgiving it on every keystroke forever. I keep one canonical list of places and one canonical set of tags, match the input against them once with a tolerant comparison, and store the reference rather than the string. After that the search becomes cheap equality, and the messy world stops being a permanent tax on every request. The cost of cleaning at entry is one pass over bad data. The cost of never cleaning is a slow query and a wrong answer, forever.

The second half of this failure is filters that were never specified. Status, date range, region, and visibility sound like implementation until someone asks what happens when a customer selects a combination nobody anticipated. Then it becomes a business decision made by an engineer who was not in the room.

The wrong thing ranks first

Two results match. Which one appears first? That question has no technically correct answer. Newer, closer, better rated, or paid. If nobody writes the rule down, the answer gets decided by whatever the sort function happened to do, and it gets decided differently on different devices, because somewhere along the way the phone started sorting.

That phone-side sort is a recurring and almost invisible bug. I have watched a client merge results that the database had already ordered correctly, comparing distances rounded to a single decimal place, which quietly puts the wrong row on top. Nothing looks broken. Nothing throws. The list is simply, repeatably wrong, and because it is wrong in a way that looks plausible, it survives review for months.

Some ordering decisions are not preferences but gates. A musician who is not open to teaching should not appear when someone is looking for a teacher, however well the name matches. A campaign the reader is not eligible for should not be shown. Budget already spent should remove the option rather than offer something the business cannot honour. Eligibility first, then order, and both in the query, so the ranking arrives correct rather than being sorted into correctness on a device that is already slow.

The right thing arrives too late

Latency needs a number and a reference device, and it needs both before anyone starts optimising. First results within four hundred milliseconds on a mid-range phone on a throttled connection is a requirement a team can design against. Fast on my own phone is how latency budgets die, because the device in your pocket during development is rarely the one in your customer's hand.

Much of the slowness is not the database at all. It is work happening in the wrong place. Pulling a wide result and filtering it on the phone, issuing several small queries to assemble one list, or merging sources in the client, all feel acceptable on good connectivity and are unusable on a weak one. Filters belong inside the query so they narrow before anything is returned. Ranking belongs inside the query so one round trip brings back the right order. And pagination should move by position rather than by page number, so that content arriving while somebody is browsing does not duplicate the rows they already saw or silently swallow the ones they have not.

Which failure you have depends on your business

The three failures are the same everywhere. Which one is expensive is not, and this is where the work actually varies.

A devotional library serving villages on unreliable connections feels the third failure hardest, because a result that takes eight seconds is a result the person never sees. A bus network booking across many separate companies feels the second, because the wrong trip appearing first sends a passenger to the wrong day. A rewards platform feels the first, and it feels it in currency rather than in patience, because showing a campaign to somebody who is not eligible, or whose budget is already spent, is money leaving the business.

So the engineering priority on any search is not a technical judgement. It is a fact about who is paying and what they lose. That has to be established before the work starts, or the team will optimise for the failure that is easiest to measure rather than the one that costs most.

Where my approach stopped working

Twice, on two very different products, I fixed search and did not measure it afterwards. I removed a broken client-side sort and moved filtering into the query, watched complaints stop, and moved on. Both times I had no idea whether the change had actually improved the three things that matter, because I had never established a baseline and never built the corpus that would have let me prove matching was correct rather than merely uncomplained about.

The fix is unglamorous. A corpus of queries with the results they must return, including misspellings and inconsistent spellings of the same place, agreed with whoever owns the business. A latency budget with a named device. A ranking rule written down by the owner rather than inferred by me. None of these are engineering tasks, which is exactly why they get skipped, and skipping them means the next round of optimisation is guesswork with a build attached.

Search looks like a box and a list. The cost is never in either of them. It is in the gap between what the person meant and what the system heard, and closing that gap starts with naming which of the three failures you are actually living in.

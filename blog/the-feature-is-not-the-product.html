The feature is not the product. The business is.

The most expensive thing I see in mobile development is a solution reused. Not stolen, not copied badly, reused by someone who genuinely believes it worked well somewhere else. It did work well somewhere else. That somewhere else had a different customer, a different failure, and a different definition of expensive. Reuse transfers all of that without telling you.

This is not a technology argument. It is a business one. Search, booking, payment, identity, and messages are not features. They are decisions, and the right shape of each decision is set by what being wrong costs in your specific business. The owner of that business is the only person who knows that number, and nobody asks them before the screens get drawn.

Search fails three ways, and businesses feel them differently

Every search I have worked on broke in one of three places. Something that should match did not, the wrong thing ranked first, or the right thing arrived too late. The diagnosis is identical across products. What differs is which of the three actually costs you.

A devotional library serving villages on a weak connection feels the third one hardest. A result that takes eight seconds is a result the person never sees, because they have walked away. A bus network booking across dozens of separate companies feels the second one, where the wrong trip appears first and a passenger commits to the wrong day. A rewards platform showing campaigns to people who are not eligible, or who have already been paid from an exhausted budget, feels the first, and it costs you money rather than patience.

Same three sentences. Completely different engineering priorities, and the priority is a business fact, not a technical one.

Booking leans toward whoever absorbs the failure

The mechanics of a reservation are largely settled. Hold the inventory, verify the payment, write the ticket together with the decrement, let the hold expire on its own, and make a repeated press return the first result. I have implemented exactly this shape for boats, for buses, for donations, and for surveys, and the reliability gain has been identical every time.

What changes is where the rules lean. A river boat with a fixed number of seats absorbs failure in public, at a dock, in front of a person holding a ticket. So it leans hard toward safety, short holds, and immediate self-release. A bus network where each company settles its own day absorbs failure in its accounts, so it leans toward separation, and one company's rush must never touch another company's numbers. A donation leans toward a single provable outcome, because a person charged twice for a donation does not retry. They leave, and they tell other people.

Payment is trust, and trust has different shapes

For payment the non-negotiable is the same everywhere. Verify on the server, never trust the device, and let a repeated press find the first result rather than creating a second charge. Every product I have shipped with money in it enforces this.

Beyond that, the trust object differs. For a donor it is the receipt, which has to arrive, has to be readable later, and sometimes has to survive a human reviewing a bank transfer. For a passenger booking from a dock with no account, it is a bill that matches exactly on both sides, down to rounding, because a mismatch means either an argument or a loss. For a platform settling dozens of independent companies, it is the separation of the books. For a rewards product, it is the wallet showing a real stored number rather than a hopeful one.

Identity depends on who the person actually is

People who travel without email need a phone number that survives reinstalls, because that number is the only thing tying them to their booking. People who arrive through a social sign-in need their profile, their chat identity, and their preferences created in one pass, or the product has a half-registered stranger in it. A company running drivers, cashiers, inspectors, and managers needs access separated by company and by responsibility, and revocation that actually takes effect rather than merely logging someone out of one device.

The same login screen solves none of these by itself. Which one you have is decided by who your customer is and what they have on them.

Messages are priced by what they are for

Every notification needs a channel order and a fallback, and the order follows the message's job. A habit has to arrive with no connection at all, which means the schedule lives on the device. A warning about a departure has to arrive on time, because an hour-late warning is misinformation. A payment needs its proof attached. A social action needs to restart a loop.

The expensive one is the fallback, because every fallback message costs money per send. Whether a confirmation is worth a text fallback, and whether marketing is, is a budget decision with your name on it. It should be signed before launch, not discovered on the first invoice.

The one question that prevents most of this

Before I design any of these, I ask the owner one question: what does being wrong cost you here? Not what should we build. What does the failure actually look like for your business, and who absorbs it.

If the answer is a passenger turned away at a dock, everything follows. If it is a person quietly gone after one failed message, everything follows. If it is a support morning, everything follows. The engineering is downstream of that answer, and that answer is not mine to invent. It is not even mine to guess at. It is the one thing I am obliged to ask for before drawing anything.

The owners I work best with are not the ones who know the answer immediately. They are the ones willing to sit with the question, because they understand that a feature built on a guessed answer will work perfectly and still be wrong for their business.
